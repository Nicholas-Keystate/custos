## What this seed carries

One designation, and six consequences that follow from placing it
in the founding law rather than anywhere else.

Under section 17's track one a governance event and a credential
issuance are the same bytes in the same form, anchored the same
way, and section 11 types the same domain as producing both.
Section 17 commits a canonical *order* for folding GEL events and
no rule for *membership*. Which anchored spans are the GEL is
therefore underivable, and three plausible derivations from
identical committed bytes yield three different Constitutions
with no diagnostic.

R15 rules the membership principle and its instrument: every span
the fold consumes as GEL is derivable from committed bytes, and
the derivation runs through a designation the founding law
commits at inception grade.

## Repair — the designation

**Ratified spans.** Cited, not edited. Track one (L2216–2222):

> **Track one — registry-form reuse.** GEL events use the
> substrate's registry event forms under their existing ilks,
> with governance semantics carried entirely by the committed law
> that interprets them. This track is the colorless base: any
> registry-capable consumer parses the events unharmed, and the
> governance reading is the adopting domain's committed act.

The bootstrap, which commits placement and not membership
(L2232–2235):

> **The bootstrap.** Track choice and, for track two, the initial
> ilk table are committed law — and a verifier must derive them
> before it can admit the first event they govern.

Its refusal, which this repair extends (L2246–2252):

> Where a verifier cannot derive the initial placement or table
> from the applicable committed founding-law referent … it
> refuses the stream — a missing rule, not missing evidence — and
> the refusal names the underivable commitment.

The genesis knot, which seals the founding law and nothing else
(L617–620):

> K0 SHALL seal C's self-addressing identifier among its
> anchoring seals, and the gAID is K0's self-addressed prefix:
> the founding law lies inside the bytes the identity digest
> ranges over.

And axiom 2's singular reference to "the GEL span" (L248–250),
which presupposes the derivation the document never states.

**The defect.** Team A folds every registry-form event anchored
in the gAID's KEL as a GEL event, so an ordinary credential
issuance becomes a governance event and the Constitution acquires
clauses nobody enacted. Team B requires a designation, finds no
rule, and invents one — the first registry inception after the
inception seal, the registry named in C, the registry whose
identifier appears in founding law. Both return well-formed
Constitutions; section 7.3's byte-identity SHALL (L1037–1038) is
violated with no diagnostic; and section 17's bootstrap refusal
does not fire, because placement *is* derivable and only
membership is not.

The defect exists **because** the reduction of the GEL to a
registry-shaped log is complete. Nothing distinguishes governance
traffic from credential traffic at the byte level, and nothing
committed supplies the distinction.

**Replacement — a clause in the founding law's committed content,
stated in section 17's bootstrap and section 4's gAID entry.**

> **Designation.** A born-governed domain's founding law SHALL
> commit the identifier of the registry that carries its
> governance event log, at the same grade as track choice: a
> clause of the founding law sealed by the genesis knot,
> derivable before the first governed event is admitted. The GEL
> is the designated registry's anchored spans and no others. An
> adopted domain designates in its later-anchored founding law,
> at that construction's confessed lesser grade.
>
> Migration of the governance registry is an enactment, judged
> under the law in force before it, exactly as a grammar
> migration is.
>
> Where a verifier cannot derive the designation from the
> applicable committed founding-law referent, it refuses the
> stream — a missing rule, not missing evidence — and the refusal
> names the underivable commitment. No positional, ordinal, or
> otherwise ambient rule supplies a designation the founding law
> did not commit.

## Ground for the designation, and against the alternatives

**Against a positional default.** The cheapest rule to write — the
first registry incepted under the gAID is the GEL — is the worst
fit, and it fails twice. It fails silently for any domain that
incepts a credential registry before its governance registry, and
the failure is a *different Constitution*, not an error. And it
cannot express migration at all: a domain that moves its
governance registry has, under a positional rule, either not
moved or ceased to exist. R15 rejects it on the record so it is
not rediscovered as the obvious shortcut, which is what the
finding asked for.

**Against a span-selection predicate (option B), for now.** A
predicate is more expressive and R15a is its cheap instance. It
is not ruled out; it is deferred, because it admits more than one
governance registry and therefore trades the singleness corollary
below for expressiveness nobody has yet exhibited a need for. A
is derivable before the first governed event, which is what the
bootstrap requires, and it leaves B available later without
rework.

**For inception grade specifically.** The designation has to be
derivable *before* the first governed event is admitted, or the
verifier must fold events to learn which events to fold. Only the
genesis knot is available at that position — it is the one
commitment that precedes the log — which is why the clause lives
in the founding law rather than in an early GEL event.

## The singleness corollary

The designation is the domain's GEL-singleness declaration, and
its enforcement is inherited rather than added.

> Two designations for one domain require either two founding-law
> byte-sets against one anchored self-addressing identifier,
> which is digest-impossible, or two key event logs for one gAID,
> which is key-tier duplicity convictable in the medium.

Nothing new enforces singleness of law, because the knot already
does. That is worth saying plainly, because the same is *not*
true one level up: registry singleness and asset integrity stand
on committed clauses adjudicated by the designated GEL's fold.
Singleness of law is grounded in physics; every other singleness
in a governed domain is conferred by clause. A reader who takes
the first for the second will expect the medium to enforce
guarantees only the law provides.

## The shadow-registry disposition

A non-designated registry claiming to carry law grounds nothing —
and the enforcement is placed where it is real.

> **The designation check enters the verification cone.** A
> finding's law head SHALL be derivable as the fold of the
> subject gAID's designated GEL: knot, founding law, designation,
> registry, fold, law head. A well-formed warranty citing the
> fold of a non-designated registry is must-reject, however
> well-signed and however well-formed its cone.

This is the eloquent-stranger vector: an artifact that passes
every check the document currently states, cites a law head
computed from a registry the domain never designated, and is
therefore evidence about a stranger's private computation rather
than about this domain's law. Without the cone-side check the
warranty is indistinguishable from an honest one.

Two boundaries hold at the edges of this rule. Exhibited use of
shadow law — a domain acting on a fold of a registry it did not
designate — converts to ordinary breach of its own committed
clauses, and is adjudicated as breach rather than as a new crime.
Private influence without committed effect is out of scope: the
standard governs what enters the record, and a domain's
uncommitted deliberations were never its subject.

## The confessed bound

One entity operating two gAIDs, each with clean key state and no
duplicity anywhere, is beyond byte-conviction by construction.
The standard convicts identifiers, not entities, and no amount of
designation discipline changes that.

The lawful mitigation is a clause, not a mechanism: founding law
may commit a sole-authority clause, which converts surfaced
linkage evidence into ordinary breach of a committed predicate.
The text should say plainly that authority singleness is
clause-grade and never physics-grade — the reverse of the GEL
singleness above — because a reader who has just been told that
singleness of law is enforced by the knot will otherwise carry
that grade one step too far.

## The fail-loud law

> Law **content** may blind under the ratified disclosure
> postures. **Designation and membership fail loud or not at
> all.** Any membership rule that can yield a proper subset of
> the GEL without firing a refusal is must-reject.

This is R15a's poison, recorded so that D is not rediscovered as
a shortcut. Membership by committed state vocabulary is cheap and
looks ambient-free, because it reads a field. Under a blinded
attribute block the field sits inside the blind, so a verifier
holding a partial disclosure computes a **shorter GEL with no
error** — a smaller Constitution, well-formed, silently missing
the clauses it was not shown. A membership rule with that
property is worse than one that refuses, because a refusal is
visible and a short fold is not.

R15 records A and D as composable if R19 lands on the `(td, ts)`
track — founding law names the registry, the state vocabulary
types the events within it — and holds the composition for that
sitting, as one design. This seed drafts A alone and does not
foreclose the composition.

## The weight guard

> Recognition and receipt weight never substitute for
> designation. A heavily-warranted fold of a non-designated
> registry is must-reject as law. Recognition adds force above
> the designation check; it never replaces it.

The guard is kernel-grade because the failure it prevents is the
one the transformation law exists to prevent: a fold acquiring
the status of law by accumulating consumers. Section 12's
consumption rule already says consumption confers evidence-weight
and never force (L1573), and no threshold of consumption by
others binds any frame that never committed to it (L1571–1573).
The weight guard is that doctrine applied to the domain's own
law, where the temptation is strongest.

## What this discharges

R1's dependency. The closed triple's first member is "the GEL
span", and the position-indexing seed records the exposure it
inherited: E's first named member had an extent the document
could not derive. With the designation committed, it can. The
foundation R1 declares now stands on bytes at every member.

## Notes for the drafting authority

Things surfaced in drafting that R15 did not name.

1. **Item 6 and section 6's disclosure posture disagree at one
   point, and the disagreement should be settled rather than
   inherited.** Section 6 rules that "a cone span withheld under
   such a profile is appraised by the ordinary pending species —
   an undisclosed span is a typed requirement, not a defect"
   (L918–920). Item 6 rules that membership fails loud or not at
   all. A withheld *founding-law* span is both: a withheld cone
   span, and the span the designation is derived from. The
   reading this seed assumes is that the two rules partition —
   evidence spans discharge as pending, and the founding-law
   referent is not an evidence span but the rule-source the
   bootstrap refusal already guards — but the text nowhere says
   so, and section 17's own refusal-boundary vectors draw the
   pending/refusal line only at the grammar grain (L2296–2299).
   The same line is now owed at the membership grain.

2. **The cone's extent grows, and the transitive-closure clause
   already reaches it.** Section 6 says the cone "SHALL contain
   every log span the finding's replay reads, across every
   citation, to the depth replay actually touches" (L872–877),
   and that "a fresh verifier needs exactly one entry point held
   in advance: the gAID" (L877–878). The designation chain is
   read by every replay, so the founding-law referent is now
   inside every cone by the existing clause rather than by a new
   one — and the single entry point still suffices, since the
   chain starts at the gAID's inception. Worth stating once at
   the site, because a producer assembling cones from the
   ratified text alone would not obviously include it.

3. **"Registry identifier" is substrate vocabulary this document
   has not adopted.** Section 4's GEL entry names a log; the
   designation names a registry. The two are the same object
   under track one and the document does not currently have a
   word for the registry qua registry. Either the clause names
   the substrate's own field, which imports vocabulary section 3
   says is cited and never restated, or it says "the log" and
   loses precision about what is being designated. A drafting
   call, flagged because the clause is a must-reject boundary and
   its subject should not be ambiguous.

4. **Adopted domains inherit a weaker designation than the seed's
   prose suggests.** Section 4 says an adopted domain's founding
   law "is as displaceable as any later anchor" (L628–629). A
   displaceable founding law carries a displaceable designation,
   so an adopted domain's GEL membership is grounded at a grade
   the seed names in one clause and does not analyze. If the
   consequence is that adopted domains cannot satisfy the
   membership principle at full grade, that is worth saying
   outright rather than leaving to the phrase "confessed lesser
   grade" to carry.

5. **Station obligations: three vectors, routed to #15.** The
   eloquent stranger — a well-formed warranty citing a
   non-designated fold, expected must-reject. The two-team
   membership pair — the finding's own Team A and Team B streams,
   expected *refusal*, not either invention. And the
   membership-underivable bootstrap refusal. They belong in #15
   beside R10's antinomy fixture and R3's regression vector, and
   this seed does not carry them.
